## The Culture Concept

### What is Culture?

Culture is a set of shared customs. 

Sometimes, culture is self-identified and intentionally reproduced.[^family] Other times, it is an external label that is implied to be unwittingly reproduced.[^poverty]

[^family]: "in my culture we value family" 
[^poverty]: "the culture of poverty"

We treat culture as a distinct category from political or religious institutions, even though it shapes and is shaped by both.

We distinguish "high" culture from "low" culture, implicitly ranking cultures against each other, even as anthropologists have argued that culture should be judged on its own terms rather than ranked at all (==cultural relativism==).

### The History of Anthropology

Culture was originally a tool used from the perspective of the colonizer to understand the colonized ("the other").

> "Culture... includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society" - E.B. Tylor[^tylor]

[^tylor]: Tylor was one of the first anthropologists in England. He developed this definition at the height of European imperialism.

There were several issues with early anthropological work:
- They relied on the accounts of others, but did not have contact with the people they were studying (==armchair anthropology==)
- They evaluated other cultures from the viewpoint that their own culture was superior (==ethnocentrism==)
- They believed that cultures evolved through distinct stages, from savagery, barbarism, to civilization (==cultural evolutionism==)

Around the 19th century, anthropologists began to travel and live among the group of people they are studying. Fieldnotes and ==participant-observation== became the dominant way of understanding other cultures.

- Franz Boas challenged cultural inferiority by showing that Inuit culture was shaped by the cold environment, and so cannot be compared to cultures without this environmental context[^env-det] (==cultural relativism==)

- Franz Boas also challenged racial inferiority by showing that immigrant head measurements differed from those of local Americans. Thus, environment, not race, must have caused the variability in traits.

- Margaret Mead showed that Samoan teenagers had an easier time transitioning from childhood to adulthood than U.S. teenagers[^nature]

- Bronislaw Malinowski showed that cultural traditions were developed as a response to specific needs, like food, safety, and stability (==functionalism==)[^func] 

- A.R. Radcliffe-Brown proposed a similar idea of ==structural functionalism==, which views society as an interconnected system whose parts work together to promote social security

[^env-det]: However, if a harsh environment explains a culture's traits, the same logic could be used to label a culture "primitive" because of its environment (environmental determinism)

[^nature]: Mead's research showed that (1) nurture could beat nature and (2) culture impacted individual emotions and personality traits.

[^func]: Functionalism eventually fell out of favor because it views cultures as stable and orderly; it is unable to explain social change. It also is unable to explain why a society develops one particular kind of social institution instead of another.

### Cultural Change

**How do cultures change?**

One view is that cultures change in a deterministic, teleological[^teleology] way. Examples of teleological explanations of culture include cultural evolution, functionalism, structural functionalism, and social evolution. ==Social evolution== theorizes that cultures go through a process of competition, accommodation, conflict, and assimilation.

[^teleology]: ==Teleology== is the explanation of phenomena in terms of purpose instead of cause. 

The problem with teleology is that culture changes in ways that we can't predict, and which can't be mapped into a linear structure. This is called the ==cultural specificity thesis==, also proposed by Boas.

### Politics of Culture

Boas had succeeded in challenging racial inferiority (above), but he was unsuccessful in promoting cultural relativism for ethnic immigrants and Black Americans. There persisted the idea that rural Black folk culture was "culturally inferior," and that the best outcome was for Black people to assimilate into white culture.

The NAACP Legal Defense and Education Fund led a legal campaign to desegregate public schools, culminating in the Supreme Court decision *Brown v. Board of Education*. The NAACP accepted Boas's argument that there is no racial inferiority, but rejected his cultural specificity thesis, for several reasons:
- Black people negatively deviated from the white population on multiple metrics (poverty, crime, disease), in a way that was correlated with cultural variables (incidence of female-headed households, divorces, fictive kin relations). Anthropologists believed that cultural change (assimilation) could change these metrics.
- The cultural specificity thesis held that culture was slow to change and not reversible. This was a problem for the NAACP's case because it could be read as relieving white society of any obligation to change the status quo. ==Social evolution==, by contrast, put the responsibility on white people to take part in speeding up assimilation.

**References**: Lee Baker. 1998. [Unraveling the Boasian Discourse: The Racial Politics of "Culture" in School Desegregation, 1944 - 1954](https://people.duke.edu/~ldbaker/documents/baker4.pdf), Transforming Anthropology 7(1): 15-32. 

### Cultural Text

Clifford Geertz is known for "Deep play: notes on the Balinese cockfight."

For context, the Balinese people are known for their "temper." They are physically graceful and they generally strive for calmness in daily life. They also have an extreme revulsion toward animality.[^animality]

[^animality]: Children are not allowed to crawl, and in adulthood they file down their canines so they will not look like animal fangs. 

Thus, intense emotions are channeled and released through social structures, such as a cockfight. Geertz shows that the cockfight is a metonym for broader society, capturing the hierarchical, status-conscious structure of Balinese life. When the stakes are larger ("deep play"), it becomes a matter of status. 

It is also a cultural text that represents society back to itself, allowing the Balinese people learn and rehearse its norms.

> "If... we go to see *Macbeth* to learn what a man feels like after he has gained a kingdom and lost his soul, Balinese go to cockfights to find out what a man, usually composed, aloof, almost obsessively self-absorbed... feels like when, attacked, tormented, challenged, insulted, and driven in result to the extremes of fury, he has totally triumphed or been brought totally low." - Clifford Geertz

**References**: 
- Eugene Kinkead and Russell Maloney. 1943. [The Balinese Temperament](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1943/09/04/the-balinese-temperament). The New Yorker.
- Clifford Geertz. 1973. [Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cock Fight](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/20024056.pdf). The Interpretation of Cultures.

## Cultural Relativism

There are several issues with cultural relativism:
1. It is essentialist. It believes that culture has a set of essential and immutable characteristics.
2. It is ahistorical. It assumes that culture doesn't change in response to interactions with other cultures.
3. It is amoral. Because cultural relativism tells us to evaluate beliefs, values, and practices based on own cultural context, it prevents us from assigning judgment (and thus morality). **When should we reconsider our morals? And when should we affirm them?**

But when done right, cultural relativism can instead promote:
1. Reflexivity
2. Cross-cultural solidarity
3. Respect for people's agency

For example, consider the narrative of the oppressed Muslim woman. This narrative was used by Bush to justify the War on Terror. In many ways, it appeals to liberal supporters of gender equality. 

However, cultural relativism tells us to reflexivity think about our own culture. For example, French women can't cover their faces. In most Western cultures, women can't expose their chests like men can. How is this different from Muslim woman? These thoughts may inspire a sense of cross-cultural solidarity for the efforts of Muslim women, as well as respect for their agency.

## Identity Formation

What is an identity? How can we make any declaration of the individual self?

While there has always been social groups with particular identities, the idea there are unique individuals is relatively new.

Historically, people have been defined relationally, not individually:
- In Bantu language, there is no word for the concept of the individual self. Selfhood is always defined through social relationships.
- In Nyoro society, a person would likely identify themselves by their agnatic clan and their mother's clan. 
- In Japan, people usually identify themselves by the relationship they are a part of. Rather than saying 'I am a typesetter,' a Japanese man is likely to say 'I am from B Publishing Group.'

### The Cartesian Individual

Rene Descartes tried doubt everything in the world to find the absolute truth. He realized:
if we can deduce that our thoughts are real, then it stands to reason that we are real. In other words: *cogito, ergo sum* ("I think, therefore I am").

> "... the soul, through which I am what I am, is entirely separate from the body... so that even if I did not have a body, my soul would continue to be all that it is."

This concept, "I think therefore I am," informed a mode of secular individualism that brought forth the idea that we all are unique individuals. 

The Cartesian philosophy brought forth three important ideas:
1. Scientific empiricism and objectivity. 
2. Individualism and the rights-bearing subject. 
3. Secular political agency and popular revolution

The idea behind ==scientific empiricism== is that the individual is able to divorce themself from the social and material context, and use their mind to interpret the world objectively. Black and brown people were increasingly seen as part of the non-human, subjective world.

Identity, as emerged in the early 18th century, was seen as a set of features that distinguishes a person or thing from others.

The idea behind the ==rights-bearing subject== was that man was entitled to certain rights, such as life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. This liberal philosophy informed many of the political movements of the time, such as the American Revolution.

Individualism has also generated a myth of the "Homo economicus"; the idea that we all seek to maximize our own wealth.

Historically, most societies were governed by a theological order. There was no social mobility. The idea that the individual can change their individual circumstances gave rise to a sense of ==political agency== and ==populism==. 

The idea that people were entitled to change their own circumstances was at the core of the French Revolution and the Haitian Revolution. 

### Identity Politics and Intersectionality

While secular political agency emerged from the idea of the autonomous liberal subject, it has also given rise to identity politics and intersectionality. ==Identity politics== says that marginalized people need to self-consciously organize around their shared identities. It states that we are not only individuals, but that we are also impacted by structural forces. ==Intersectionality== is the idea that structural power intersects to shape people's structural positions.

Anthropologists focus on how social contexts shapes how we identify and who we believe ourselves to be. In some ways, this rejects the idea of individualism. Yet, through cultural relativism, anthropology also affirms the individual's agency. 

Our identities are the medium through which culture change and through which cultures persist.

### Hijra Identity

**Question**: Are hijras agents of gender subversion, or are they reinscribing gendered categories through their desires and practices? 

==Hijras== are a recognized third gender in South Asia. Hijras are men who sacrifice their genitalia to a goddess in return for the power to confer fertility. They are respected for this ritual role, but also stigmatized for their ambiguous sexuality.

However, Hijra identity is not just about being a "third gender." Hijra identity is not something one has, but something that one performs through pursuing ==izzat== (respect and recognition). Thus, hijra identity is not a series of attributes, but instead a performative and relational phenomenon.

==Gender performativity== (innocent mode of signification) is an implicit, everyday act. ==Gender performance== (intentional mode of signification) is a conscious, intentional effort to mark a gendered identity. There is not a firm line between gender performativity and performance. When hijras perform their ritual, it is both performative and a performance. 

Reddy rejects the etic[^etic] analysis that hijras are challenging the sexual binary. Her emit analysis shows that hijras subscribe to an essentialist understandings of gender, focused around dress, manner, and sexual position.

[^etic]: An ==etic== reading refers to the study of a culture or a language by people who do not belong to that culture. This contrasts with an ==emic== reading, which analyzes a culture by using the language of the people in this culture.

However, hijras also had agency in a number of ways. First, hijras play with and parody gender. Hijras and other kotis[^3] did not unequivocally think of themselves as women. They often emphasized their non-binary status by performing hand claps and lifting their saris to expose genitalia (or lack thereof). They also dress their saris differently than women, intentionally emphasizing that they are different.

Gayatri Reddy. 2005. [With Respect to Sex: Negotiating Hijra Identity in South India](https://transreads.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/2019-03-17_5c8e1614a8b9c_gayatri-reddy-with-respect-to-sex-negotiating-hijra-identity-in-south-india.pdf).

[^3]: A kothi is a man who takes on an effeminate role and is the receptive partner in sexual intercourse.

## Doing Ethnography

The goal of ethnography is the production of knowledge. However, "knowledge production" is a very modern and particular way of thinking about knowledge. It came from a Cartesian empirical framework to improve society with said knowledge.

Ethnography is the close, qualitative, empirical study of a community's everyday practices and rituals. The researcher embeds themselves in the community that they are studying through ==participant observation==. Participant observation requires
the researcher to develop a working, reciprocal relationship with the people they are studying.

While anthropologists usually don't strive for objectivity, they do strive for a perspective that is less determined by their position as a researcher or outsider.  

Ethnographers take field notes, which may contain:
- Transcription of dialogue
- Description of body language, tone, and other characteristics
- Description of surroundings
- Account of an encounter
- Analysis and context

In the 1980s and 90s, anthropology faced a "crisis of representation." The field began to question that the ethnographer could objectively describe the cultures without the bias. Furthermore, people being studied obviously perform differently.  

But an anthropologist's unfamiliarity with the culture also allows them to better question and contextualize things that may be taken for granted. This is called ==inside-outsider perspective==.

In contemporary anthropology, there has been efforts to push back against the colonial and supremacist history of ethnography: 
- Most cultural anthropologists subscribe to the idea of "==situated knowledge==," which is the idea that all knowledge is situated, partial, and incomplete.[^race]
- "Studying up" refers to ethnographic research on elite people and privilege spaces. Some anthropologists take this approach to question and cast doubt into the words of the powerful interlocutors they work with.
- "Engaged anthropology" involves using anthropology to support particular communities.
- "Co-performative witnessing" is an approach that situates the ethnographer as a participant in the community, and not just an observer. The trials that affect that interlocutors also, in some way, affect the ethnography.

[^race]: For example, it has commonly been accepted that race is a social construct. But anthropologists have been open to exploring how there is a material or biological reality to living in a white body or a Black body.
